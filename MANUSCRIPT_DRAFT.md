## Abstract

**Background.**
* Snakebite envenoming is a climate-sensitive neglected tropical disease. Sub-Saharan Africa bears nearly half of its global burden.
* Snakebite is consistently seasonal. Yet recent studies in India and Ghana concluded that seasonality, rather than rainfall or temperature, drives risk.
* Those models adjusted for seasonality separately from climate. If the seasonal cycle is itself generated by lagged climate, such adjustment removes the climate effect.
* Whether snakebite seasonality *is* lagged climate has never been tested.

**Methodology.**
* We analysed 60,515 snakebite cases reported through Ghana's District Health Information Management System (DHIMS-2) from 261 districts, January 2020 – November 2025.
* Climate inputs were monthly district-level rainfall, relative humidity and temperature (NASA POWER) and vegetation (NDVI).
* Model: negative-binomial generalized linear mixed models with a population offset and district-within-region random effects.
* Distributed-lag non-linear models estimated exposure–lag–response associations over 0–[RESULT: L] months, with a pre-specified lag-selection strategy.
* We tested whether lagged local climate reproduces the seasonal cycle captured by month terms, using a placebo test: climate series reassigned from districts in a different rainfall regime, 100 permutations.
* We separated each climate variable into its seasonal climatology and its anomaly.
* We compared unimodal, transition and bimodal districts, classified from each district's own rainfall.
* We estimated climate-attributable cases and evaluated out-of-sample predictive skill for 2025.

**Principal findings.**
* The crude incidence was 32.4 per 100,000 per year: 65.8 in unimodal districts, 35.1 in the transition zone and 25.7 in bimodal districts.
* Lagged local climate reproduced [RESULT: ≈100%] of the seasonal signal and outperformed every placebo climate series (p [RESULT: <0.01]). Only [RESULT: ≈11%] of seasonal variation was unexplained by climate.
* Rainfall was associated with risk only after a delay, peaking at lag [RESULT] months in unimodal districts and lag [RESULT] in bimodal districts (cumulative RR [RESULT]).
* Humidity and minimum temperature acted within 0–1 months (RR [RESULT]). High maximum temperatures were associated with lower same-month risk.
* Incidence peaked 1–2 months after rainy-season onset, at [RESULT]% above baseline in unimodal and transition districts.
* An estimated [RESULT]% of cases were attributable to non-optimal climate.
* Adding lagged climate improved one-month-ahead prediction (CRPS reduced by [RESULT]%).

**Conclusions/Significance.**
* The seasonality of snakebite in Ghana is largely lagged local climate. Treating seasonality as a confounder hides the climate effect.
* Climate acts with measurable, regime-specific delays of one to three months.
* These delays provide usable lead time for climate-informed antivenom pre-positioning and community alerts in Ghana and similar West African settings.

**Keywords:** snakebite envenoming; climate; distributed lag non-linear model; generalized linear mixed model; rainfall onset; early warning; Ghana; West Africa

## Author summary

Snakebite kills and disables tens of thousands of people in Africa every year, mostly poor farmers in rural areas. People often say bites rise "in the rainy season". However, nobody had measured *how long after* rain, humidity or heat the risk goes up. That delay matters, because it tells health services how much warning they have to move antivenom to where it will be needed.

We used six years of monthly snakebite reports from every district in Ghana together with satellite climate data. Earlier studies concluded that snakebite follows "the season" rather than the weather. We show that, in Ghana, the season largely *is* the weather of previous months: each district's own past climate reproduced its snakebite season, while climate borrowed from the other half of the country could not. Rain does not raise snakebite risk in the same month. Risk rises one to three months later, and the delay is longer in the dry savanna north than in the wetter south. Bites surged in the one to two months after the rains started, when farmers clear and plant their fields. Humid and warm nights were linked to more bites almost immediately, while very hot days were linked to fewer. Using past climate improved forecasts of the next month's snakebite cases.

These findings could help Ghana and neighbouring countries plan antivenom supplies and warn communities ahead of high-risk periods.

## 1. Introduction

Snakebite envenoming is one of the most neglected causes of death and disability in the tropics. Recent geostatistical estimates suggest that at least 2.1 million, and possibly up to 7 million, envenomings occur each year. After adjustment for under-reporting, these cause between 274,000 and 513,000 deaths, and sub-Saharan Africa accounts for about 45% of both [[1](https://journals.plos.org/plosmedicine/article?id=10.1371/journal.pmed.1005201)]. These figures are far higher than the earlier global estimate of at least 421,000 envenomings and 20,000 deaths per year [[2](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pmed.0050218)]. In 2017 the World Health Organization re-listed snakebite as a category A neglected tropical disease. Its 2019 strategy aims to halve snakebite deaths and disability by 2030, a goal that depends on getting antivenom, trained staff and community awareness to the right places at the right times [[3](https://www.who.int/publications/i/item/9789241515641)]. In Ghana, snakebites are recorded through the District Health Information Management System (DHIMS-2) of public health facilities, and analyses of these data have shown that outbreaks can go undetected [[4](https://doi.org/10.11604/pamj.2021.40.131.28217)]. Across West Africa, many victims turn first to traditional medicine, so facility-based figures underestimate the true burden [[5](https://doi.org/10.1155/2024/8357312)]. In the savanna north, the West African carpet viper (*Echis ocellatus*) causes most severe envenoming, and bites occur mostly on farmland among men of working age [[6](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pntd.0007221)].

Snakebite is widely described as seasonal, and there are good biological and social reasons to expect its seasonality to be driven by climate. Snakes are ectotherms whose surface activity, foraging and dispersal depend on ambient temperature and moisture. Rainfall triggers vegetation growth, followed over weeks to months by pulses of seeds, insects and rodents, the principal prey of many medically important vipers [[7](https://doi.org/10.1007/s13364-018-0377-x)]. Human exposure follows the same clock. In rain-fed farming systems, land clearing, planting and weeding begin with the onset of the rains and bring barefoot or poorly protected farmers into contact with snakes [[5](https://doi.org/10.1155/2024/8357312),[6](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pntd.0007221)]. Heavy rain and humidity extremes may also drive snakes into houses and compounds. Each of these pathways implies a different delay between a climatic event and a bite, ranging from days for snake activity to months for prey dynamics, and the pathways may act in opposite directions at different lags. Any analysis that relates snakebite only to same-month climate is therefore likely to miss, or even reverse the sign of, real climatic effects.

The empirical evidence is limited and inconsistent. A 2025 systematic review of 35 spatial and temporal analyses found that temperature and humidity were mostly positively associated with snakebite risk and drought negatively. Some studies reported negative associations, however, and very few came from sub-Saharan Africa [[8](https://doi.org/10.1093/trstmh/trae131)]. Delayed effects have been examined only occasionally. In Costa Rica, incidence rose by about 24% per degree Celsius above average temperature. It was positively associated with temperature one month earlier and negatively with rainfall eleven months earlier, and it increased during both phases of the El Niño–Southern Oscillation [[9](https://doi.org/10.1126/sciadv.1500249)]. In California, bites declined after drought and increased after wet 18-month periods [[10](https://doi.org/10.1080/15563650.2018.1508690)]. In Colombia, rainfall drove incidence only where the dry season was marked [[11](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pntd.0010270)]. Most recently, a spatial case-crossover analysis of lagged precipitation and temperature in India concluded that snakebite deaths were "primarily influenced by seasonality rather than precipitation or temperature" [[12](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.sste.2025.100738)].

These studies share an important design choice. Seasonality is treated as a confounder: it is removed through calendar-month terms, harmonic functions, case-crossover time strata or temporal random effects, and only then is the effect of climate assessed. This is appropriate when seasonal variation in snakebite arises from causes unrelated to climate. In a rain-fed, ectotherm-driven system, however, the seasonal cycle of snakebite may itself be generated by lagged climate, directly through snake ecology and indirectly through the rain-timed agricultural calendar. Adjusting for seasonality then removes most of the effect it is meant to isolate, and estimates of climate effects are biased towards the null by over-adjustment. On this view, a conclusion that snakebite is driven by "seasonality rather than climate" may describe the model rather than the disease. To our knowledge, no study has tested whether the seasonality of snakebite is itself explained by lagged local climate.

Ghana illustrates the problem and offers an unusually good setting to test it. Regional analyses of surveillance and community data show that bites peak in the rainy and farming seasons [[4](https://doi.org/10.11604/pamj.2021.40.131.28217),[6](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pntd.0007221)]. Yet a recent nationwide Bayesian spatio-temporal analysis of district-level surveillance data for 2020–2025 included temporal and space–time random effects. It found humidity and temperature, but not rainfall, to be associated with risk [[13](https://www.medrxiv.org/content/10.64898/2026.07.12.26357875v1.full)]. A strongly rain-seasonal disease with no detectable rainfall effect is consistent with over-adjustment. Ghana also contains contrasting rainfall regimes within a single national surveillance system. The Guinea and Sudan savanna of the north has one rainy season followed by a long dry season, whereas the forest and coastal zones of the south have two rainy seasons, with a transition belt between them [[14](https://ugspace.ug.edu.gh/items/88e8caf1-cdc8-4d5d-9135-697dd61c6932)]. If snakebite seasonality is climatic, each district's seasonal cycle should be reproduced by its own lagged climate but not by the climate of a district in a different regime, and this contrast provides a natural falsification test.

In this study we used 72 months of surveillance data, from January 2020 to December 2025, from all 261 districts of Ghana for two purposes: to test whether the seasonality of snakebite is explained by lagged local climate, and to quantify the delayed and non-linear effects of rainfall, humidity, temperature and vegetation. Using negative-binomial generalized linear mixed models with a population offset and sex as a covariate, our primary aim was to test whether lagged local climate reproduces the seasonal cycle captured by conventional calendar-month adjustment. We used climate reassigned from a different rainfall regime as a placebo, and quantified the share of seasonality that is climatic. We further estimated exposure–lag–response associations with distributed-lag non-linear models and separated the effects of normal seasonal climate from those of unusual weather. We compared lag structures between unimodal, transition and bimodal districts, and estimated the number of snakebites attributable to climate. Finally, we evaluated whether lagged climate improves out-of-sample prediction and so provides lead time for antivenom planning.

## 2. Methods

### 2.1 Study setting

Ghana, with a population of about 34 million in 2025, is divided into 16 regions and 261 metropolitan, municipal and district assemblies. Ghana's rainfall changes from two rainy seasons in the south to one in the north [[14](https://ugspace.ug.edu.gh/items/88e8caf1-cdc8-4d5d-9135-697dd61c6932)]. The Guinea and Sudan savanna of the north has a single rainy season, mainly from May to October, followed by a long dry season during which the dry Harmattan wind blows. The forest and coastal zones of the south have a major and a minor rainy season. Because this change is gradual and does not follow administrative boundaries, we classified each district by its own rainfall rather than by its region. For each year from 2020 to 2024, a district was counted as having two rainy seasons when its lowest monthly rainfall in July–August fell below 80% of both its first peak (April–June) and its second peak (September–October). Districts with two rainy seasons in four or five of the five years were classed as bimodal (192 districts), those with two seasons in no more than one year as unimodal (47 districts), and those with two seasons in two or three years as a transition zone (22 districts), because their pattern varies from year to year. The classification was stable: changing the 80% threshold to anywhere between 75% and 90% reclassified between 3 and 15 districts. Unimodal districts lie in the Upper East, Upper West, North East and most of the Northern Region; the transition zone runs through the Savannah, northern Bono East and Oti Regions, with a few districts in Northern, Volta and Bono; and bimodal districts make up the rest of the south. In bimodal districts the major season peaks around June and the minor season around October (Fig 2). The West African carpet viper (*Echis ocellatus*) causes most severe envenoming in the northern savanna [[6](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pntd.0007221)]. A national compendium describes the other medically important snakes of Ghana, their venom activity and the clinical presentations they cause. It also notes that health-facility records rarely characterise the venom or the species responsible [[15](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pntd.0011050)]. [Fig 1](#fig1) shows the study area, with the districts shaded by rainfall regime, the 16 regions named and the location of Ghana in West Africa. District boundaries were taken from the geoBoundaries database [[16](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0231866)], whose Ghana file, sourced from the Ghana Statistical Service, contains 260 districts. The boundaries were matched to the surveillance districts by name, with 30 spellings reconciled by hand; the crosswalk is in the public repository. The maps were drawn in Python with the matplotlib library [[17](https://doi.org/10.1109/MCSE.2007.55)] and the shapely geometry package (https://shapely.readthedocs.io), using the script `analysis/fig1_map.py`.

### 2.2 Study design and data

This was a nationwide ecological panel study, with a time-series cross-sectional design, of monthly snakebite counts in every district of Ghana from January 2020 to December 2025, a period of 72 complete months.

**Health data.** Snakebite cases were obtained from the District Health Information Management System (DHIMS-2) of the Ghana Health Service, the national electronic database through which health facilities report routine service data in aggregated form. Earlier regional analyses have also used it [[4](https://doi.org/10.11604/pamj.2021.40.131.28217),[19](https://doi.org/10.1093/trstmh/trae113)]. Within DHIMS-2, new outpatient snakebite cases are entered through the monthly outpatient department (OPD) morbidity form. Admitted cases are reported through the in-patient morbidity and mortality form, and snakebite deaths are held in a separate cause-of-death database [[19](https://doi.org/10.1093/trstmh/trae113)]. The data comprise the monthly number of snakebite cases in each of the 261 districts, by sex, from January 2020 to December 2025, following the 2018 district boundaries [AUTHOR: confirm whether the extraction combined the OPD and in-patient forms or used the OPD form alone, and give the DHIMS-2 reporting rate for 2020–2025]. A case is a new attendance at a reporting health facility that was recorded as snakebite. Because the data are aggregated, they carry no individual identifiers and record neither the snake species nor the presence or severity of envenoming [[15](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pntd.0011050)]. Bites treated only by traditional healers, or at facilities that do not report to DHIMS-2, are not captured [[5](https://doi.org/10.1155/2024/8357312)].

**Population data.** Annual district population estimates for 2020–2025 were used as the offset, so that the models describe incidence rates rather than counts [AUTHOR: state the source, for example the Ghana Statistical Service projections from the 2021 Population and Housing Census]. For the sex-specific models, each district's population was divided between males and females using the national shares of the 2021 Population and Housing Census, 49.3% male and 50.7% female [[20](https://3news.com/wp-content/uploads/2021/09/2021-PHC-Provisional-Results-Press-Release_210921-1.pdf)].

**Climate and vegetation data.** Monthly climate for each district was obtained from the NASA Prediction of Worldwide Energy Resources (POWER) project [[18](https://power.larc.nasa.gov/docs/methodology/meteorology/)], whose meteorological fields derive from the MERRA-2 reanalysis [[21](https://doi.org/10.1175/JCLI-D-16-0758.1)] on a grid of about 0.5° × 0.625°. The variables were precipitation (monthly mean, mm/day), relative humidity at 2 m (%), and mean, maximum and minimum air temperature at 2 m (°C), for January 2019 to November 2025, which gives a full lag history for cases in 2020 [AUTHOR: confirm the 2019 extraction and state whether values were extracted at district centroids or area-weighted]. Climate for December 2025 was not available and was estimated from each district's own December record for 2020–2024. Vegetation greenness (NDVI) was obtained from [AUTHOR: product, for example MODIS MOD13A3, with a verified reference] and averaged over each district for the same period. For sensitivity analyses at finer resolution, rainfall was also taken from CHIRPS version 2.0 at 0.05° [[22](https://doi.org/10.1038/sdata.2015.66)], and temperature and humidity from ERA5-Land at 0.1° [[23](https://doi.org/10.5194/essd-13-4349-2021)].

### 2.3 Derived exposures

Several exposures were derived from the monthly climate data to separate the regular seasonal cycle from unusual weather, to describe the timing of the rains and to capture the combined effect of heat and moisture.

For each climate variable $x$ in district $i$ and month $t$, with calendar month $m(t)$, the climatology $\bar{x}_{i,m(t)}$ was defined as the mean of that variable in that district and calendar month over the study period. It describes the conditions a district normally experiences at that time of year, and therefore carries both the seasonal cycle and the differences in climate between districts. The anomaly, $a_{it} = (x_{it} - \bar{x}_{i,m(t)})/s_x$, where $s_x$ is the standard deviation of $x_{it} - \bar{x}_{i,m(t)}$ across all district-months, measures how much wetter, more humid or hotter a given month was than usual for that district. Because the anomalies are centred within each district and calendar month, they are free of the seasonal cycle by construction. Comparing the effects of climatology and anomaly therefore distinguishes the influence of the normal season from that of unusual weather.

The onset of the rainy season was defined, for each district and year, as the first month from February onwards in which mean rainfall reached at least 2 mm/day, roughly 60 mm in the month. February was used as the starting point so that rain falling late in the previous rainy season was not counted as a new onset. Because any threshold is to some extent arbitrary, onset was also defined with thresholds of 1.5 and 3 mm/day and with a cumulative-rainfall criterion in sensitivity analyses. A false start was recorded when onset was followed within two months by a month with less than 1 mm/day of rain, a pattern that can disrupt planting and prolong land preparation. Each district-month was then indexed by the number of months since onset in that district and year, from three months before onset to six months after, so that snakebite incidence could be aligned to the start of the rains rather than to the calendar.

Two further variables were derived for secondary analyses. The diurnal temperature range, the difference between the monthly mean maximum and minimum temperatures, describes how much a district cools at night relative to the heat of the day, which affects the hours during which snakes are active. The vapour-pressure deficit summarises the drying power of the air by combining temperature and humidity. It was calculated as $\mathrm{VPD} = e_s(T)\,(1 - \mathrm{RH}/100)$, where $T$ is the monthly mean temperature in °C, $\mathrm{RH}$ the relative humidity in per cent and $e_s(T) = 0.6108\,\exp\{17.27\,T/(T + 237.3)\}$ the saturation vapour pressure in kPa [[24](https://www.fao.org/4/x0490e/x0490e00.htm)]. The vapour-pressure deficit also offers a single measure of heat and moisture, which is useful because relative humidity and maximum temperature are strongly correlated in these data and are difficult to separate in the same model.

### 2.4 Statistical analysis

#### 2.4.1 Descriptive analysis

The seasonal profiles of snakebite and climate were described separately for unimodal, transition and bimodal districts. For each rainfall regime, we summed the reported cases in each calendar month across districts and years and plotted them alongside the mean monthly rainfall, relative humidity and temperature of the same districts. This shows when snakebite peaks relative to the rainy seasons and whether the timing differs between regimes. To relate the snakebite season to the start of the rains rather than to the calendar, we also plotted incidence by months since rainy-season onset, expressed relative to each district's mean.

The distribution of monthly counts was examined to choose the count model. We calculated the mean and variance of district-month counts and their ratio. A ratio well above 1 indicates overdispersion, meaning more variation than a Poisson model allows, and supports a negative-binomial model. We also calculated the proportion of district-months with no reported cases, overall and for each district. A larger share of zeros than the negative-binomial model predicts would point to a zero-inflated or hurdle model, and these alternatives were assessed in the sensitivity analyses.

#### 2.4.2 Base model

The monthly number of snakebite cases $Y_{it}$ in district $i$ and month $t$ was modelled with a negative-binomial generalized linear mixed model with a log link:

$$Y_{it} \sim \mathrm{NB}(\mu_{it}, \theta), \qquad \mathrm{Var}(Y_{it}) = \mu_{it} + \mu_{it}^2/\theta,$$

$$\log \mu_{it} = \log(\text{Pop}_{it}) + \beta_0 + s_{g(i)}(m_t) + \gamma_{y(t)} + u_{r(i)} + v_i, \qquad u_r \sim N(0,\sigma_u^2), \quad v_i \sim N(0,\sigma_v^2).$$

Each component was chosen both for what the data showed and for its statistical properties.

*Negative-binomial distribution.* The counts were strongly overdispersed, with a variance 8.8 times the mean (Section 3.1). A Poisson model assumes that the variance equals the mean, and under overdispersion it understates standard errors and overstates the significance of every effect [[25](https://cameron.econ.ucdavis.edu/racd2/)]. The negative binomial arises as a Poisson model whose rate varies between observations according to a gamma distribution. This adds the term $\mu_{it}^2/\theta$ to the variance, and the model reduces to the Poisson as $\theta \to \infty$ [[25](https://cameron.econ.ucdavis.edu/racd2/)]. It was preferred to the quasi-Poisson model because it has a full likelihood, needed for comparing models by AIC and for lag selection. Its variance also grows with the square of the mean, which suits data in which large counts are rare but highly variable [[26](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18051645/)]. Although 31% of district-months recorded no cases, the negative binomial already predicts many zeros at low means, $P(Y_{it}=0) = \{\theta/(\theta+\mu_{it})\}^{\theta}$, so zeros alone do not require a different model. Zero-inflated [[27](https://doi.org/10.2307/1269547)], hurdle [[25](https://cameron.econ.ucdavis.edu/racd2/)] and NB1 alternatives were fitted in sensitivity analyses, and observed zeros were compared with zeros simulated from the fitted model [[28](https://CRAN.R-project.org/package=DHARMa)].

*Population offset.* District populations differed 16-fold. With $\log(\text{Pop}_{it})$ as an offset, $\mu_{it}/\text{Pop}_{it} = \exp(\eta_{it})$, where $\eta_{it}$ is the rest of the linear predictor. The model therefore describes incidence rates, and $\exp(\beta)$ is the incidence rate ratio for a covariate with coefficient $\beta$ [[25](https://cameron.econ.ucdavis.edu/racd2/)].

*Seasonality and year effects.* Unimodal districts peaked in July, whereas bimodal districts peaked in May with a second rise in September–October (Section 3.2), so calendar-month effects $s_{g(i)}(m_t)$ were allowed to differ by rainfall regime. This is the conventional adjustment for seasonality in time-series studies of environment and health [[29](https://doi.org/10.1093/ije/dyt092)]. It is also the reference against which the primary analysis tests whether lagged climate can reproduce the seasonal cycle (Section 2.4.5). Year effects $\gamma_{y(t)}$ absorb national changes in reporting, care-seeking and long-term trends that could otherwise be confounded with climate [[29](https://doi.org/10.1093/ije/dyt092)]. These include the disruption of 2020, when outpatient attendance in Ghana fell during the COVID-19 pandemic [[30](https://doi.org/10.1136/bmjopen-2022-061608)].

*District and region random intercepts.* District incidence ranged from 0 to 277.5 per 100,000 per year and regional incidence from 5.4 to 111.9 (Section 3.1). These differences reflect persistent, unmeasured characteristics such as snake fauna, land use, access to care and completeness of reporting. Random intercepts account for these differences and for the repeated measurement of each district [[31](https://experts.mcmaster.ca/scholarly-works/1146888)], and they borrow strength across districts so that districts with few cases are not estimated in isolation [[32](https://assets.cambridge.org/97805218/67061/copyright/9780521867061_copyright_info.pdf)]. With 72 months per district, the climate effects are identified mainly from changes over time within districts, which protects them against confounding by stable district characteristics [[33](https://eprints.ncrm.ac.uk/id/eprint/3317/)].

*Autocorrelation.* Monthly counts were correlated within districts (lag-1 correlation 0.20). Where residual autocorrelation remained, a district-level first-order autoregressive term was added, with $\mathrm{Corr}(\varepsilon_{it}, \varepsilon_{i,t+k}) = \rho^{|k|}$ [[34](https://doi.org/10.32614/RJ-2017-066)]. Spatial correlation was examined in the residuals and addressed in a sensitivity analysis with a spatial model [[35](https://arxiv.org/abs/1601.01180)] (Section 2.4.11).

*Sex.* Males accounted for 58% of cases, consistent with greater exposure during farm work [[6](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pntd.0007221)]. For the analyses by sex, the data had one row per district, month and sex $k$, and the model became

$$\log \mu_{ikt} = \log(\text{Pop}_{ikt}) + \beta_0 + \delta\,\text{male}_k + s_{g(i)}(m_t) + \gamma_{y(t)} + u_{r(i)} + v_i,$$

so that $\exp(\delta)$ is the male-to-female incidence rate ratio. Interactions between sex and the climate terms tested whether climate effects differ between the sexes.

*Data and estimation.* Models were fitted to 18,636 district-months, after excluding district-months in which reporting appeared to have failed rather than cases to have been absent. These were one district with no reports in any month, one with no reports after 2023 and five district-years with no reports in districts that otherwise averaged at least 10 cases a year. Parameters were estimated by maximum likelihood, with the random effects integrated out by the Laplace approximation [[31](https://experts.mcmaster.ca/scholarly-works/1146888),[34](https://doi.org/10.32614/RJ-2017-066)].

#### 2.4.3 Distributed-lag non-linear models

* Each climate variable entered the base model as a cross-basis [36].
* The cross-basis is the tensor product of:
  * a natural cubic spline over the exposure range; and
  * a natural cubic spline over lags 0 to $L$ months, with knots equally spaced on the log scale, which allows more flexibility at short lags.
* Lags were computed within districts across year boundaries.
* Because raw relative humidity and maximum temperature are strongly correlated (r = −0.80):
  * the main model included rainfall and one temperature metric;
  * humidity was assessed in an alternative model;
  * variance inflation factors are reported.
* We report:
  * lag-specific relative risks (RR) at the 90th and 99th percentiles of exposure versus the median (or the minimum-risk value);
  * the cumulative RR summed over all lags;
  * the overall cumulative exposure–response curve.

#### 2.4.4 Lag selection strategy (pre-specified)

To avoid the biases of single-lag testing and cross-correlation in strongly seasonal data, we pre-specified the following strategy:

1. **Maximum lag from mechanism.** The main analysis uses $L$ = 6 months, covering activity (0–1), agricultural-calendar (0–2) and vegetation–prey (1–4) pathways. Sensitivity analyses use 3, 9 and 12 months.
2. **Joint estimation.** All lags were estimated jointly within the cross-basis. We did not use separate single-lag models, cross-correlation or stepwise selection.
3. **Grid selection.** $L$ and the spline degrees of freedom were chosen from a pre-specified grid: $L \in \{3, 6, 9, 12\}$ × exposure df ∈ {2, 3, 4} × lag df ∈ {2, 3, 4}. Two criteria were used:
   * the Akaike information criterion, with all candidates fitted to identical observations;
   * leave-one-year-out cross-validation scored by the logarithmic score and continuous ranked probability score (CRPS).

   The most parsimonious model within 2 AIC units, or within 1 SE of the best cross-validated score, was retained.
4. **Penalized confirmation.** A penalized DLNM [37] with $L$ = 12, fitted by restricted maximum likelihood, served as a data-driven check of the lag shape.
5. **Common specification.** The same $L$ and df were applied to all variables and zones, so lag curves are directly comparable.
6. **Falsification.** Future climate (lags −1 and −2) was added to the final model. No association was expected.

We report full lag curves and cumulative effects rather than a single "selected" lag.

#### 2.4.5 Primary analysis: is seasonality lagged climate?

We compared four nested NB GLMMs, all including district and region random intercepts, year effects and the population offset, fitted to identical observations:

| Model | Seasonality represented by |
|---|---|
| M0 | none |
| M1 | zone-specific calendar-month terms (the conventional adjustment) |
| M2 | own lagged climate cross-bases (rainfall, RH, Tmin, Tmax, NDVI; lags 0–L), no month terms |
| M3 | M1 + M2 |

1. **Share of seasonality reproduced by climate:** $(D_{M0} - D_{M2}) / (D_{M0} - D_{M1})$, where $D$ is the deviance. We also compared AIC and leave-one-year-out cross-validated scores, so that models with different numbers of parameters are compared fairly.
2. **Residual non-climatic seasonality:** the deviance gain of M3 over M2, as a share of the seasonal deviance. This captures factors such as school holidays, market and harvest events, and reporting cycles.
3. **Placebo (falsification) test.**
   * Each district's climate series was replaced by that of a randomly chosen district from the *other* rainfall regime, keeping calendar time aligned, and M2 was refitted. This was repeated 100 (final: 1,000) times.
   * If climate explains seasonality only because any seasonal series would, placebo climate should fit as well as own climate.
   * The permutation p-value is the proportion of placebo fits with deviance ≤ the own-climate fit.
4. **Temporal placebo.** Each district's climate was replaced by its own climate from a different year, which preserves the seasonal shape but breaks year-to-year alignment. This tests whether inter-annual climate variation adds to explaining snakebite beyond the average seasonal cycle.

Because rain-fed farming is timed by rainfall, the farming calendar lies on the causal pathway from climate to bites (a mediator, not a confounder). The climate effect estimated here is therefore the total effect, including behavioural responses to climate.

#### 2.4.6 Climatology versus anomaly decomposition

* We refitted the DLNM with the standardised anomaly $a_{it}$ in the cross-basis and the climatology $\bar{x}_{i,m(t)}$ as a separate term. This is a within–between decomposition [33].
* The anomaly terms estimate the delayed effect of unusual weather independently of normal seasonality.
* The climatology term captures the seasonal and spatial climate gradient.

#### 2.4.7 Heterogeneity by rainfall regime

* Lag structures were compared between unimodal, transition and bimodal districts (Section 2.1).
* Method: fit zone-specific models, reduce each to its overall cumulative curve and its lag curve at a fixed exposure, and pool them by multivariate meta-analysis (`mixmeta`) [38].
* Heterogeneity was summarised with Cochran's Q and I².
* A likelihood-ratio test of a zone × cross-basis interaction gave a complementary test.

#### 2.4.8 Rainy-season onset

* An event-study mixed model replaced calendar-month effects with months-since-onset indicators (−3 to +6; reference −3), estimated separately by zone, adjusting for year and district random effects.
* In secondary analyses, onset month (and false start) predicted:
  * the timing of the annual peak (linear mixed model); and
  * the total seasonal count (negative-binomial GLMM).

#### 2.4.9 Attributable burden

* Attributable numbers and fractions of cases were computed from the final DLNM [39], relative to the minimum-risk value of each exposure.
* Results are given by zone, region and year, with 95% empirical confidence intervals from 1,000 Monte-Carlo simulations.

#### 2.4.10 Predictive validation

* Models were trained on 2020–2024 and evaluated on January–November 2025, and in rolling-origin cross-validation.
* Three nested models were compared:
  * (A) seasonal terms + random effects;
  * (B) A + lagged climate restricted to lags ≥ 1, to mimic real-time use;
  * (C) B + onset terms.
* Metrics: mean absolute error, CRPS, 90% prediction-interval coverage, PIT histograms, and sensitivity and specificity for high-incidence months (above the district's 75th percentile).

#### 2.4.11 Sensitivity analyses

1. Maximum lag 3, 9 and 12, and alternative df.
2. NB1, zero-inflated and hurdle negative-binomial models.
3. Exclusion of the 24 flagged district-months, and of 2020 (COVID-19).
4. Fourier seasonal terms instead of month effects.
5. CHIRPS and ERA5-Land exposures.
6. Climate-grid-cell random effect or cluster-robust SEs (for districts sharing NASA POWER cells).
7. Monthly interpolated population offset.
8. Bayesian spatial model with BYM2 district effects in R-INLA [35].

#### 2.4.12 Diagnostics and software

### 3.2 Seasonality

* **Unimodal districts.** Cases rose from March, with the first rains, and peaked in July (2,356 cases), two months before the rainfall peak in August–September (8.4 mm/day).
* **Bimodal districts.** The main peak was in May (3,919 cases), during the major rainy season, which peaks in June (8.8 mm/day). A secondary rise in September–October coincided with the minor season, which peaks in October (8.4 mm/day) (Fig 2).
* **Transition zone.** Cases peaked in May (594) and stayed high through August, while rainfall stayed above 6 mm/day from June to September, with no clear mid-season dip.
* **Onset timing.** The median onset of the rainy season was April in unimodal districts and March in transition and bimodal districts.
* **Post-onset surge.** Two months after onset, incidence relative to the district mean reached 1.58 in unimodal districts, 1.73 in the transition zone and 1.25 in bimodal districts, returning to about 1.0 by four to five months after onset.

## 4. Discussion

**¶1 Principal findings.** [RESULT-driven summary answering each objective in order. Lead with the headline: the seasonal cycle of snakebite in Ghana is largely reproduced by each district's own lagged climate. Placebo climate from a different rainfall regime cannot reproduce it, and only about a tenth of seasonality is non-climatic. Then: rainfall acts with a delay of 1–3 months that is longer in unimodal districts; humidity and night temperature act immediately.]

**¶1b Seasonality is not a confounder of climate here.**
* The conclusion that snakebite is driven by "seasonality rather than precipitation or temperature" [12] and the null rainfall effect in Ghana [13] both come from models that remove seasonality before assessing climate.
* Our results show that, in Ghana, most of that seasonality *is* lagged climate.
* Seasonal adjustment is necessary when seasonality reflects non-climatic drivers. Where climate generates the season, it is over-adjustment that biases climate effects towards the null.
* We recommend that climate–snakebite studies report models with and without seasonal adjustment, together with a placebo-climate test, before concluding that climate is unimportant.

**¶2 Mechanisms.**
* Short-lag effects of humidity and minimum temperature are consistent with increased nocturnal snake activity and refuge-seeking in humid, warm conditions.
* The negative same-month association with high Tmax suggests thermal avoidance at temperatures above the species' optimum, as the systematic review anticipated [8].
* The 1–2-month post-onset surge matches the agricultural calendar: land preparation, planting and weeding.
* The longer rainfall lag in unimodal districts fits a delayed vegetation–rodent prey pulse after a single prolonged dry season [7].
* [Discuss the NDVI lag pattern: cover vs prey resource.]

**¶3 Comparison with previous studies.**
* *Costa Rica:* temperature at lag 1 agrees; the long rainfall lag (11 months) is outside our window [9].
* *Sri Lanka:* low humidity was associated with more bites, opposite in sign to our finding; this may reflect wet-zone saturation [40].
* *Colombia:* rainfall mattered where dry seasons are marked; Ghana's unimodal districts are the analogue [11].
* *California:* precipitation acted at long lags [10].
* *Ghana nationwide analysis:* our results reconcile its null rainfall finding with descriptive rainy-season peaks [13]. Rainfall's effect is delayed and seasonal, and temporal and space–time random effects can absorb it.
* *India:* "seasonality rather than climate" [12]. Within case-crossover time strata, the seasonal climate signal is removed by design, leaving only short-term anomalies, which we also find to have small effects. The two results are consistent once the climatic origin of seasonality is recognised.

**¶4 Methodological contribution.**
* To our knowledge, this is the first study to test whether snakebite seasonality is itself explained by lagged climate, using a placebo-climate falsification design. This moves "seasonality" from a nuisance term to an estimand.
* It is also, to our knowledge, the first nationwide exposure–lag–response analysis of snakebite in sub-Saharan Africa. [AUTHOR: confirm from the full text of [12] whether a DLNM was used before claiming "first DLNM for snakebite".]
* It separates seasonal climatology from weather anomalies and compares lag structures between rainfall regimes.
* The pre-specified lag-selection strategy (joint estimation, information-criterion and cross-validated selection, penalized confirmation, falsification with future lags) avoids the seasonal-confounding artefacts of cross-correlation and single-lag testing. The same strategy is transferable to other envenomings and climate-sensitive NTDs.

**¶5 Public-health implications.**
* A lag of 1–3 months gives health services a practical window. Antivenom and health-worker training can be pre-positioned in northern districts about two months after onset, and in southern districts earlier, with a second, smaller window in September–October.
* Linking the model to Ghana Meteorological Agency seasonal and onset forecasts could create an operational early-warning system, in line with the WHO 2030 roadmap.
* High-burden districts with poor treatment access [13] should be prioritised.

**¶6 Strengths and limitations.**
* *Strengths:*
  * nationwide coverage of all 261 districts over 71 months;
  * a hierarchical count model with a population offset;
  * flexible exposure–lag–response estimation;
  * explicit control of seasonal confounding;
  * out-of-sample validation;
  * extensive sensitivity analyses.
* *Limitations:*
  * facility-based surveillance under-counts bites treated by traditional healers, and completeness may vary between districts. District random effects absorb stable differences, but not changing ones.
  * The ecological design cannot attribute exposure to individuals.
  * Climate data are coarse (districts share grid cells), although results were robust to finer products [RESULT].
  * Six years are too few to study inter-annual drivers such as ENSO, or long (> 12-month) population lags.
  * DHIMS-2 lacks species, envenomation severity and outcome data.
  * The COVID-19 period may have altered care-seeking.
  * Population denominators are projections.

**¶7 Future research.**
* Species- and severity-specific outcomes.
* Linkage with antivenom stock and distribution data.
* Longer series to capture ENSO and Atlantic teleconnections.
* Prospective piloting of a climate-informed early-warning tool.
* Extension to neighbouring West African countries with similar rainfall regimes.

---

## 5. Conclusions

* The seasonal cycle of snakebite in Ghana is largely lagged local climate, so treating seasonality as a confounder hides the effect of climate.
* Snakebite risk responds to climate with measurable, regime-specific delays.
* Rainfall acts one to three months ahead and through the onset of the rainy season. Humidity and warm nights act within the same month, and extreme daytime heat suppresses risk.
* Modelling these delays explains why concurrent rainfall appears unrelated to a strongly rain-seasonal disease. It also provides lead time that can be used for climate-informed snakebite preparedness.
